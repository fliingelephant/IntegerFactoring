# F55 candidate: formal inverse-pair parity collapses, arithmetic square parity remains open

## Status and decisive question

**Status:** proof-only kill-first candidate for the first global decoder proposed
after P62. It has not had a hostile audit or a proof-blind reconstruction.

P62 produces many public relations

\[
a_jb_j=N k_j+1,
\qquad
a_jb_j\equiv1\pmod N,
\qquad
1\le a_j,b_j<N.
\tag{1.1}
\]

The proposed decoder does not inspect one coordinate at a time. It pools many
relations, builds an endpoint-incidence matrix over \(\mathbb F_2\), and asks
for a subset in which every public endpoint label occurs an even number of
times. The selected integer product is then a visible square, so its square
root is tested as a root of \(1\bmod N\).

This is a real whole-batch operation. The theorem below shows that, after
directly screening every submitted self-loop, it adds no factoring
information. The reason is specific: modular inversion is an involution. The
underlying simple nonloop graph is a matching, while the multigraph kernel is
generated by even selections of parallel nonloop edges and by self-loops.
The theorem does not close arithmetic square-class pooling, smoothness,
chronological use of the descent states, lattices, continued fractions, hidden
periods, or any nonlinear transcript decoder.

The closest earlier boundary is P01. P01 says that a universally sound linear
sketch cannot compress arbitrary rational square classes. The result below is
different. It analyzes the exact special generator supplied by P62 and proves
that the nonloop part of one tempting public parity kernel has image \(1\)
modulo \(N\). Its full image is generated only by submitted self-loop roots,
each of which is already directly screenable.

## 1. Formal endpoint-parity decoder

Let \(N\ge3\) be odd. Let

\[
U_N=\{x\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}:\gcd(x,N)=1\}.
\]

For \(x\in U_N\), let \(\iota(x)\) be the least positive representative of
\(x^{-1}\bmod N\). Then \(\iota\) is an involution of \(U_N\).

Suppose a batch contains relations indexed by \(j=1,\ldots,m\), with

\[
b_j=\iota(a_j).
\tag{1.2}
\]

Repeated relations are allowed. Choose a subset \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\).
For every public integer label \(x\in U_N\), let \(e_x(S)\) be the total
number of endpoint occurrences of \(x\) among the selected pairs
\((a_j,b_j)\), counted with multiplicity. Assume

\[
e_x(S)\equiv0\pmod2
\quad\text{for every }x.
\tag{1.3}
\]

Then the integer

\[
Y_S=\prod_{j\in S}a_jb_j
\]

is the visible square

\[
Y_S=X_S^2,
\qquad
X_S=\prod_{x\in U_N}x^{e_x(S)/2}.
\tag{1.4}
\]

Since every selected pair has product \(1\bmod N\), one has

\[
X_S^2\equiv1\pmod N.
\tag{1.5}
\]

The question is whether this root can be nontrivial.

## 2. Exact collapse on the inversion matching

Partition \(U_N\) into the orbits of \(\iota\). Every orbit is either

1. a two-element set \(\{x,\iota(x)\}\), with \(x\ne\iota(x)\); or
2. a singleton \(\{z\}\), where \(z^2\equiv1\pmod N\).

Every relation in a two-element orbit has the same unordered endpoint pair.
Condition (1.3) therefore selects an even number of relations from that orbit.
If it selects \(2h\) of them, their contribution to \(X_S\bmod N\) is

\[
(x\iota(x))^h\equiv1\pmod N.
\tag{2.1}
\]

A self-loop \((z,z)\) already has even endpoint parity by itself. If the
subset contains \(h_z\) such loops, their contribution to the visible square
root is \(z^{h_z}\). Consequently,

\[
\boxed{
X_S\equiv
\prod_{z\in U_N:\ z^2\equiv1\bmod N}z^{h_z}
\pmod N.
}
\tag{2.2}

Thus the full kernel image lies in the subgroup generated by the self-inverse
labels already present in the batch. Products of several such labels can be
new root values; equation (2.2) does not say that the output must equal one
submitted label.

For each such label \(z\), direct screening of

\[
\gcd(z-1,N),
\qquad
\gcd(z+1,N)
\tag{2.3}
\]

either gives a proper factor or proves \(z\equiv\pm1\pmod N\). The latter
claim uses that \(N\) is odd: the two factors \(z-1,z+1\) have no common odd
prime divisor. After all self-loops pass this screen without factoring,
every factor in (2.2) is \(1\) or \(-1\), so

\[
X_S\equiv\pm1\pmod N.
\tag{2.4}
\]

Therefore the endpoint-incidence parity decoder creates no new factoring
information. Before screening, several submitted self-loops can multiply to a
new root value. But any nontrivial submitted self-loop already factors \(N\)
through (2.3). On the branch where all direct screens fail, every pooled output
is \(\pm1\).

This conclusion holds for any number of relations, any adaptive method for
choosing them, and any algorithm for finding kernel vectors of the formal
incidence matrix. It uses only the submitted inverse-pair interface.

## 3. Why arithmetic square parity is different

Formal label parity is not the same as true integer square parity. Distinct
public labels can share rational prime factors, and one product can be a square
without repeating any endpoint label.

For example, with \(N=15\), the inverse pair

\[
(a,b)=(2,8)
\]

satisfies

\[
ab=16=4^2\equiv1\pmod{15}.
\]

The root \(4\) is nontrivial and

\[
\gcd(4-1,15)=3.
\]

The formal incidence vector of this single nonloop edge is not zero. Its
success comes from the arithmetic factorization \(2\cdot8=2^4\), not from an
even repetition of the labels \(2\) and \(8\).

For a general batch, let \(C\) be the endpoint-label incidence matrix over
\(\mathbb F_2\): column \(j\) records the parity of the two occurrences
\(a_j,b_j\). Let \(V\) send each public label to its rational-prime valuation
vector modulo two. The arithmetic relation matrix is \(VC\); equivalently,
its \(j\)-th column is the rational square class

\[
[a_j]+[b_j]=[a_jb_j]
\quad\text{in }\mathbb Q^\times/(\mathbb Q^\times)^2.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Formal pooling searches \(\ker C\), while true square-class pooling searches
\(\ker(VC)\). Thus

\[
\ker C\subseteq\ker(VC),
\tag{3.2}
\]

and the inclusion can be strict, as the \(N=15\) example shows. Finding a
vector in the larger kernel can use trial division over a factor base,
smoothness, partial relations, or a stronger arithmetic representation. P01
says that a universally sound lower-dimensional exact linear sketch cannot
replace this square-class information for arbitrary inputs. It does not rule
out special low-rank or smooth endpoint laws produced by the descent.

## 4. Algorithmic consequence

The first proposed whole-transcript decoder is closed:

\[
\text{repeat public endpoint labels}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{parity kernel}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{square root of }1
\]

has nonloop contribution \(1\). Its remaining contribution is generated by
submitted self-loop roots. Direct screening of those roots either factors
\(N\) first or reduces every pooled output to \(\pm1\).

The surviving decoder must use information that the inversion matching
forgets. The smallest concrete retry is arithmetic square-class pooling of the
actual integers \(a_j,b_j\). It would need an all-input theorem that
polynomially many descent relations have enough efficiently recognizable
dependencies and that the resulting square root is nontrivial with
inverse-polynomial probability. No such theorem is proved here.

Another retry can use the chronological equations

\[
u_i v_i=N u_{i+1}+1,
\]

including the decreasing magnitudes and shared states. A lattice,
continued-fraction, stabilizer recursion, or hidden-period construction must
state exactly what it extracts from those linked equations. Merely taking the
binary cycle space of the inverse-pair multigraph cannot work: even parallel
nonloop selections decode to \(1\), and loop generators were already directly
screenable. Graph constructions that use chronology, integer vertex values,
or added relations are outside this statement.

This result is not a factoring lower bound and does not close F26. It separates
one empty form of amortization from the arithmetic amortization that could
still change the algorithm.
