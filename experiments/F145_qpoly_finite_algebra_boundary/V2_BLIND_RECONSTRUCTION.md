## I. Uniform nonunits

Let (J=\operatorname{Jac}(A)). Since (A) is a finite-dimensional
commutative algebra, it is Artinian and (J) is nilpotent. An element of
(A) is a unit if and only if its image in (A/J) is a unit. Indeed, a lift
of an inverse modulo (J) gives a product of the form (1-j), and (1-j)
is invertible for nilpotent (j). The quotient map has equal-size fibers,
so a uniform element of (A) maps to a uniform element of

\[
A/J\simeq\prod_{i=1}^s \mathbb F_{r^{e_i}}.
\]

The coordinates in this product are independent and uniform. A tuple is a
unit exactly when all its coordinates are nonzero. Therefore

\[
\Pr[a\text{ is a nonunit}]
=1-\prod_i(1-r^{-e_i}).
\]

The union bound gives

\[
1-\prod_i(1-r^{-e_i})\leq\sum_i r^{-e_i}.
\]

Also, every (e_i\geq1) and
(\sum_i e_i=\dim_{\mathbb F_r}(A/J)\leq d). Hence

\[
\sum_i r^{-e_i}\leq \frac{s}{r}\leq\frac d r.
\]

This also proves the nilpotent assertion: the radical changes the number of
lifts of every semisimple element by the same factor, so it does not change
the nonunit probability at all.

Now let (\mathcal A) be finite free of rank (d) over
(R=\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z). CRT and freeness give a bijection

\[
\mathcal A\simeq\mathcal A_p\times\mathcal A_q
\]

under which a uniform element has independent uniform local components.
Reduction commutes with the multiplication matrix and its determinant:

\[
D(a)\bmod r=\det(m_{a_r}).
\]

For a finite-dimensional algebra over a field, (m_x) is nonsingular if
and only if (x) is a unit. Thus (r\mid D(a)) exactly when (a_r) is a
nonunit. A proper gcd with (N=pq) occurs exactly when one, but not both, of
the local components is a nonunit. Independence gives

\[
\Pr[1<\gcd(D(a),N)<N]
=\alpha_p(1-\alpha_q)+\alpha_q(1-\alpha_p)
=\alpha_p+\alpha_q-2\alpha_p\alpha_q.
\]

Using the local bounds proves (3). For an adaptive sequence, conditional
freshness is sufficient: after every transcript, the next probe has the
same local bounds. Applying the tower property and a union bound proves
(4); independence between different times is not needed.

Finally, quasipolynomial (M) and (d) satisfy (Md=2^{o(n)}). If
(\min(p,q)\geq2^{cn}) for some fixed (c>0), then

\[
Md(1/p+1/q)\leq 2^{o(n)}\,2^{1-cn}=2^{-\Omega(n)}.
\]

## II. Monogenicity and generic Krylov rank

A finite etale algebra has a decomposition

\[
A\simeq\prod_i\mathbb F_{r^{e_i}}.
\]

For each (m), let (t_m) be the number of degree-(m) factors. To make
one element generate the product, assign distinct monic irreducible
degree-(m) polynomials to those (t_m) factors and choose a root in each
factor. The resulting tuple has minimal polynomial equal to the product of
all the assigned irreducibles, hence of degree (d), and therefore
generates (A).

There are enough irreducibles when (d<r). For (m=1) there are (r).
For (m\geq2), the number (I_r(m)) of monic irreducibles of degree (m)
is the number of elements of exact degree (m) in
(\mathbb F_{r^m}), divided by (m). Elements of smaller degree lie in
proper subfields, so the elementary union bound

\[
mI_r(m)\geq r^m-\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}r^j\geq r
\]

is enough here. Since (mt_m\leq d<r), it follows that
(t_m<r/m\leq I_r(m)). This proves (5).

There is also a direct probability proof for (7). Let

\[
\Omega=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb F_r\text{-alg}}
(A,\overline{\mathbb F}_r).
\]

Etaleness gives (|\Omega|=d). After scalar extension, an element (a)
has the coordinate list ((\sigma(a))_{\sigma\in\Omega}). The algebra
generated by (a) has full dimension (d) exactly when these (d) values
are pairwise distinct. Equivalently,

\[
\operatorname{rank}[1,a,\ldots,a^{d-1}]=d
\]

exactly when no two geometric coordinates collide. For each distinct pair
(\sigma,\tau), the map (a\mapsto\sigma(a)-\tau(a)) is a nonzero
(\mathbb F_r)-linear map. Its kernel has at most (r^{d-1}) elements, so
its collision probability is at most (1/r). Hence the sharper estimate

\[
\Pr[\operatorname{rank}K(a)<d]
\leq {d\choose2}/r
\]

implies (7), including its cap at (1).

For a uniform element of the rank-(d) CRT algebra, the two reductions are
uniform. If both local Krylov ranks equal (d), they are equal to each
other. Consequently a mismatch is contained in the union of the two
local rank-defect events. Applying (7), with its harmless looser constant,
gives

\[
\Pr[\operatorname{rank}K(a_p)\ne\operatorname{rank}K(a_q)]
\leq d(d-1)(1/p+1/q).
\]

This argument never compares the two factor-degree partitions, so they may
be different. Conditional freshness again permits the same bound after
each adaptive transcript, and a union bound proves (8a).

Under (8b),

\[
M d(d-1)(1/p+1/q)
\leq 2Md^2/\min(p,q)
=2^{-\Omega(n)}.
\]

If (M) and (d) are each quasipolynomial, then (Md^2=2^{o(n)}), so the
stated special case follows. This explicit rank condition is essential;
the conclusion is not asserted for characteristic-scale (d).

## III. Genuine local Frobenius

Squarefreeness gives

\[
A_r\simeq\prod_{e\in\lambda_r}\mathbb F_{r^e}.
\]

On a degree-(e) field factor, the normal basis theorem supplies a basis

\[
\theta,\theta^r,\ldots,\theta^{r^{e-1}}.
\]

The (r)-power Frobenius cyclically permutes this basis. Its characteristic
polynomial is therefore (T^e-1). Characteristic polynomials multiply over
products, which proves (9).

To prove injectivity of (\lambda\mapsto H_\lambda), factor over the
integers:

\[
T^e-1=\prod_{m\mid e}\Phi_m(T).
\]

The exponent of (\Phi_m) in (H_\lambda) is the number of parts of
(\lambda) divisible by (m). Starting with the largest possible part and
descending, these exponents uniquely recover the multiplicity of every
part. Thus different partitions give different integer polynomials.

Let (C(T)=\chi_F(T)\in R[T]). A division-free characteristic-polynomial
algorithm is valid over (R), and reduction commutes with its universal
ring operations. Hence

\[
C\bmod p=H_{\lambda_p}\bmod p,
\qquad
C\bmod q=H_{\lambda_q}\bmod q.
\]

For a partition with (s) parts, the coefficient (\ell_1)-norm of
(H_\lambda) is at most (2^s\leq2^d). Therefore every coefficient of a
difference (H_\lambda-H_\mu) has absolute value at most (2^{d+1}).
Since (\lambda_p\ne\lambda_q), injectivity supplies a nonzero coefficient
difference. The size promise in (10) ensures that this difference remains
nonzero modulo both (p) and (q).

This gives an explicit extraction algorithm. Enumerate the integer
partitions (\lambda) of (d). For every coefficient of
(C-H_\lambda), take its gcd with (N). When
(\lambda=\lambda_p), every coefficient difference is zero modulo (p),
while at least one is nonzero modulo (q). The corresponding gcd is (p).
The symmetric choice (\lambda_q) similarly exposes (q). Thus the
enumeration must return a proper factor.

The number of partitions is (\exp(O(\sqrt d))). Characteristic-polynomial
computation, coefficient construction, subtraction, and gcd computation
otherwise use polynomially many operations in (d) and the (n)-bit
modulus. For (d=(\log n)^{O(1)}), the complete bit complexity is
quasipolynomial.

Conversely, knowing (p,q) lets one work in each local quotient. Compute
(h_p=X^p\bmod(f,p)) and (h_q=X^q\bmod(f,q)) by binary powering. In the
power basis, the local Frobenius sends (X^j) to (h_r^j). CRT-gluing all
matrix entries constructs (F). Thus, on the explicit partition-mismatch
promise, construction of this genuine glued map carries enough information
to factor (N). The public map (x\mapsto x^N) has exponent (pq), not
the required local exponent (p) or (q), and is not generally the same
map.

## IV. Monomial powers and order conditions

Put (Q=r^e). The nonzero elements of (K=\mathbb F_Q) form a cyclic
group of order (Q-1). Besides the solution (x=0), the equation
(x^E=x) is (x^{E-1}=1) in that cyclic group. It has
(\gcd(E-1,Q-1)) solutions. This proves (11) and (12), including (E=1).

For additivity, take the unique (m\in\{1,\ldots,Q-1\}) congruent to (E)
modulo (Q-1). The functions (x^E) and (x^m) agree on all of (K).
Under the inherited setting here, (r) is prime. If (x^m) is additive,
then

\[
(X+Y)^m-X^m-Y^m
\]

vanishes on (K^2). Its degree in each variable is less than (Q), so it
is the zero polynomial. Thus all intermediate binomial coefficients of
row (m) vanish modulo (r). By the base-(r) digit criterion (or Lucas'
theorem), this occurs exactly when (m=r^j). Since (m<Q), one has
(0\leq j<e). Conversely every (r^j)-power map is additive. This proves
(13). It also shows why (r) must mean the local prime: for a general
prime power (r=\ell^a), the corresponding statement would use powers of
(\ell).

The same cyclic-group argument shows that the monomial is the identity on
all of (K) exactly when (E\equiv1\pmod{Q-1}), proving (14).

For (E=N^k), reduction modulo (p-1) gives (p\equiv1), hence

\[
(pq)^k\equiv q^k\pmod{p-1},
\]

which proves (15). When (q) is a unit modulo (p-1), the defining
property of multiplicative order proves (16). If (e\mid k), then
(p^k\equiv1\pmod{p^e-1}), so

\[
(pq)^k\equiv q^k\pmod{p^e-1},
\]

which proves (17).

Rearranging (12) gives the fixed-density condition exactly. Equations
(13)--(17) identify the congruences a successful monomial power must hit;
they contain no claim that a quasipolynomial adaptive exponent menu cannot
hit one asymmetrically.

## V. The first selective Hasse coefficient

For (1\leq k<p), use

\[
k{N\choose k}=N{N-1\choose k-1}.
\]

Neither (p) nor (q) divides (k). Reducing the identity modulo each
prime shows that both primes divide ({N\choose k}), and hence (N) does.
This proves (19).

At (k=p), the same identity modulo (q) shows that
(q\mid{N\choose p}), because (p) is invertible modulo (q). On the
other hand,

\[
{pq\choose p}
=q\prod_{i=1}^{p-1}\frac{pq-i}{i}
\equiv q(-1)^{p-1}\equiv q\not\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

The last sign is (1) for odd (p), and is also (1) in
characteristic (2). Thus (p\nmid{N\choose p}), proving (20). Therefore
(p) is the first Hasse index whose scalar coefficient distinguishes the
two CRT components, and its gcd reveals (q).

For a balanced semiprime, (p\geq2^{\Omega(n)}). Every fixed
quasipolynomial numerical cutoff

\[
L(n)=2^{(\log n)^{O(1)}}=2^{o(n)}
\]

satisfies (L(n)<p) for all sufficiently large (n). All coefficients in
a truncation through (L(n)) therefore vanish modulo the full modulus.
This conclusion concerns the numerical initial interval only. It does not
apply to sparse binary-encoded large indices or to a compressed range
computation.
