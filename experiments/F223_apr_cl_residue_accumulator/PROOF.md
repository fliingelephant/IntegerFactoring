# Proof of the F223 APR/CL residue-accumulator candidate

## 1. Local characters and one orbit

Fix a prime `q` not dividing `N`. The group

\[
G_q=(\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z)^\times
\]

is cyclic. The equivalence of (A1) and (A2) is the definition of the
subgroup generated by `N mod q`.

If `r=N^i mod q`, applying any character gives

\[
\chi(r)=\chi(N)^i.
\]

Conversely, suppose one common `i` makes this equality true for every
character. Then

\[
\chi(rN^{-i})=1
\]

for every character of `G_q`. Characters separate points in a finite
abelian group. Hence `rN^{-i}=1`, which proves `r=N^i mod q`.

If one exponent works, two exponents `i,j` give the same residue exactly
when

\[
N^{i-j}=1\pmod q.
\]

This is equivalent to `h_q` dividing `i-j`, so the exponent set is one
class modulo `h_q`.

For a finite bank, the problem is now the ordinary system

\[
i\equiv i_q(r)\pmod {h_q}.
\]

The generalized Chinese remainder theorem says this system is soluble
exactly when every pair agrees modulo the gcd of its two moduli. This is
(A5).

This proof also shows the information boundary. A separate existential
quantifier at every auxiliary prime gives local membership only. It does
not give the shared exponent used by a consolidation step.

## 2. The Cohen-Lenstra shared primary exponents

This section imports the named primary theorem. It does not reprove the
Gauss-sum and Jacobi-sum identities.

Cohen-Lenstra condition (6.4) states, for every primary prime `p` dividing
their auxiliary `t` and every prime divisor `r` of `N`, a relation in the
group `1+p Z_p`. Its exponent is `ell_p(r)`. Their discussion after
Theorem 6.3 identifies this exponent through the `p`-adic logarithm and
explains its primary uniqueness.

Their Theorem 7.8 begins with a passing Gauss-sum congruence for a
character `chi` of `p`-power order. Under condition (6.4), it proves

\[
\chi(r)=\chi(N)^{\ell_p(r)}.
\tag{1}
\]

The crucial point is the order of the quantifiers. The exponent
`ell_p(r)` was fixed by `p` and `r` before the character and auxiliary
prime were varied. Thus every character with the same primary support sees
one compatible primary exponent.

The character families chosen for an auxiliary prime generate its full
character group. Section 1 then converts (1) into the corresponding residue
congruence. Cohen-Lenstra Theorem 6.3 combines all primary exponents by CRT
and proves

\[
r\equiv N^i\pmod S
\]

for one exponent modulo their public `t`.

Algorithm 11.1 describes the central stage that proves these hypotheses or
proves compositeness. Algorithm 12.1 then performs the final orbit trial
divisions. A failed Jacobi-sum identity is only a compositeness proof in
these algorithms. It is not stated to be a rational factor.

The original APR construction has the same consolidation geometry in a
different presentation. In its Section 4, equations (4.5) and (4.6)
assemble primary index guesses. Steps C.1-C.3 reconstruct a candidate
prime divisor modulo the product of the Euclidean primes. Step C.4 tests
the candidate as an integer divisor. APR Remark 4.1 makes the important
scope distinction: a composite that passes the extraction tests is factored
in consolidation, while typical composites are rejected earlier.

## 3. Combining the orbit with the beta-two modulus

The low-bit certificate and (C3) give

\[
p\equiv b\pmod {2^\tau},
\qquad
p\equiv1\pmod M.
\tag{2}
\]

The true prime `p` proves that these congruences are compatible. The
generalized CRT therefore returns one residue `s_0 mod L_0`, where

\[
L_0=\operatorname{lcm}(2^\tau,M).
\]

By (C5), some `i_*` in `I` satisfies

\[
p\equiv N^{i_*}\pmod S.
\tag{3}
\]

Equations (2) and (3) prove that the pair of congruences (C8) is compatible
for `i_*`. The algorithm tests this condition with gcds and extended
Euclid, so it retains `i_*`. Its CRT residue satisfies

\[
p\equiv s_{i_*}\pmod L,
\qquad
L=\operatorname{lcm}(L_0,S).
\tag{4}
\]

We also need coprimality. Since `N` is odd, its prime divisors do not divide
`2^tau`. If a prime `rho` divided both `M` and `N`, applying (C3) to
`r=rho` would give `rho | rho-1`, a contradiction. Thus `gcd(L_0,N)=1`.
The hypothesis `gcd(S,N)=1` now gives

\[
\gcd(L,N)=1.
\tag{5}
\]

Choose each `s_i` as a canonical residue. A gcd with `N` can already return
a factor. On the true branch, if `L>p`, the canonical residue is `p` and
the gcd returns `p`. Equality `L=p` is excluded by (5). Otherwise

\[
0<s_{i_*}<L<p<N,
\qquad
p\equiv s_{i_*}\pmod L.
\tag{6}

This is the known-residue interface used by Gao-Feng-Hu-Pan. Its cost is
QP when

\[
L\ge N^{1/4}/A(n).
\]

There are at most QP candidate exponents. The explicit encoding hypotheses
make every modular exponentiation, generalized CRT, and gcd screen
numerical QP. One known-residue call per retained exponent therefore
preserves numerical-QP cost. At least the true call succeeds. This proves
Theorem C.

The proof uses only the divisibility certificate `M | r-1`. It never asks
for one rational element of exact common order `M`.

## 4. Fixed-bank obstruction from primes in progressions

Fix the finite bank `Q` and its odd product `R`. If `R=1`, the claimed odd
obstruction is empty. Assume `R>1`.

The residue class `-1 mod R` is reduced. The prime number theorem for the
fixed arithmetic progression gives

\[
\pi(2X;R,-1)-\pi(X;R,-1)
\sim \frac{X}{\varphi(R)\log X}.
\tag{7}
\]

The right side tends to infinity. Hence there is `X_0(Q)` such that every
`X>=X_0(Q)` has at least two primes in `(X,2X)` in that residue class.
Choose two and call them `u<v`.

Since `u>X` and `v<2X`,

\[
u<v<2X<2u.
\]

Thus the pair is balanced. For each odd `q` in the bank,

\[
u\equiv v\equiv-1\pmod q,
\qquad
N=uv\equiv1\pmod q.
\]

The subgroup generated by 1 is `{1}`. Since `q` is odd, `-1` is not 1.
Neither hidden prime is in the orbit. Modulo 2, every odd number is 1, so
that row is vacuous.

This proves Theorem D for every sufficiently large scale, not only along
an unspecified subsequence. It also explains why the bank must be fixed:
the modulus `R` is fixed when the prime-number theorem in a progression is
applied.

## 5. Generic independent-residue tails

For fixed `i` and `q`, exactly one of the `q-1` possible values of `U_q`
equals `a_q^i`. This proves (E2). Independence of the sampled residues
gives independence of the indicators.

For `lambda>0`,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E e^{\lambda K_i}
&=\prod_{q\in Q}\mathbb E e^{\lambda I_{q,i}}\\
&=\prod_{q\in Q}
\left(1+\frac{e^\lambda-1}{q-1}\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
\]

Markov's inequality applied to `exp(lambda K_i)` proves (E3).
Using `1+x<=e^x`,

\[
\mathbb E e^{\lambda K_i}
\le \exp((e^\lambda-1)\mu).
\]

For `k>=mu`, the choice `e^lambda=k/mu` gives (E5).

Likewise,

\[
e^{\lambda W_i}
=\prod_{q\in Q}q^{\lambda I_{q,i}},
\]

so

\[
\mathbb E e^{\lambda W_i}
=\prod_{q\in Q}
\left(1+\frac{q^\lambda-1}{q-1}\right).
\tag{9}
\]

The event `P_i>=Y` is `W_i>=log Y`. Markov's inequality and optimization
over `lambda` prove (E7). A union bound over candidate exponents requires no
independence between them.

The fixed-bank theorem shows why this exact probability calculation cannot
be promoted to an all-input number-theoretic distribution theorem.

## 6. Exact primary-prime-2 anchor counts

The group `F_u^times` is cyclic of order `m=u-1`. The endomorphism

\[
x\longmapsto x^E
\]

has a kernel of size

\[
g_u=\gcd(E,m).
\]

Therefore the equation `x^E=1` has exactly `g_u` solutions. A uniform
local component gives (F4).

Write a generator as `z`. The equation `x^E=-1` becomes

\[
Ek\equiv m/2\pmod m.
\tag{10}
\]

A linear congruence has solutions exactly when

\[
g_u\mid m/2,
\]

and in that case it has exactly `g_u` solutions. Since `u` is odd, the
fibres over 1 and -1 are disjoint. This proves (F8). The proof at `v` is
identical.

A uniform unit modulo `N=uv` has independent uniform CRT components.
For two events with probabilities `alpha_u,alpha_v`, the union and XOR
probabilities are respectively

\[
\alpha_u+\alpha_v-\alpha_u\alpha_v
\]

and

\[
\alpha_u+\alpha_v-2\alpha_u\alpha_v.
\]

This proves (F5) and (F6).

It remains to bound the kernel sizes by integer structure. If an integer
divides both `E` and `u-1`, it divides both `2E=N-1` and `u-1`. Reduction
modulo `u-1` gives

\[
N-1=uv-1\equiv v-1\pmod {u-1}.
\]

Consequently

\[
g_u\mid\gcd(N-1,u-1)
=\gcd(v-1,u-1)=d.
\tag{11}
\]

The symmetric statement gives `g_v|d`. Also

\[
d\mid(v-1)-(u-1)=v-u.
\tag{12}

If `v-u<=H`, then `g_u,g_v<=H`. A union bound for the two local sign
events gives (F10). Balance gives

\[
u,v=2^{n/2+O(1)},
\]

which is the displayed exponential form.

If a separate setup gives `g_u=g_v=d`, substitute these equalities into
(F5) to obtain (F11). Equation (11) alone gives divisibility, not equality.

## 7. Exact witness reconstruction

The post-hoc verifier uses trial division through the integer square root
to certify that 32987 and 32993 are prime. It computes

\[
32987\cdot32993=1088340091.
\]

The divisors of 66 are enumerated. Adding 1 and retaining primes gives

\[
2,3,7,23,67.
\]

Their product is 64722, while

\[
32989^2<N<32990^2.
\]

The exact local rows are:

\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|l}
q&N\bmod q&u\bmod q&v\bmod q&\langle N\rangle\\ \hline
2&1&1&1&\{1\}\\
3&1&2&2&\{1\}\\

The local script checks these displayed finite statements. Since it was
written after discovery, the proof is the displayed exact arithmetic, not
a preregistered search claim.

## 8. Why this is not yet Las Vegas factoring

Theorem C is a terminal interface. It says what a compatible certificate
would buy. It does not generate that certificate.

Theorem D defeats every fixed finite bank. Theorem E is only a generic
model and cannot control adversarial integer residue classes. Theorem F
shows that the most direct uniform anchor at primary prime 2 is
exponentially sparse on bounded-gap balanced inputs.

Finally, a failed APR/CL test proves compositeness but need not expose a
coefficient whose gcd with `N` is proper. Therefore restarting after
compositeness failures is not a Las Vegas factoring proof. The missing
theorem remains an inverse-QP certified-progress law at the integer
source.
