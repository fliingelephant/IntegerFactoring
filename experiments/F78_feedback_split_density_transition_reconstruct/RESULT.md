Throughout, write an element of \(G\) as \((x_p,x_q)\), and let
\(\pi_p,\pi_q\) be the two CRT projections.

## 1. Structure of a separator-free subgroup

The kernel of \(\pi_p|_H\) consists of the elements \((1,y)\in H\). Every
nonidentity element of this kernel is a positive separator. Since \(H\)
contains no positive separator, this kernel is trivial. The map

\[
\pi_p|_H:H\longrightarrow H_p
\]

is therefore an isomorphism: it is injective by the preceding argument and
surjective by the definition of \(H_p\). The same proof applies to
\(\pi_q|_H\). Hence

\[
|H|=|H_p|=|H_q|=h.
\]

In particular,

\[
\theta=\pi_q|_H\circ(\pi_p|_H)^{-1}:H_p\longrightarrow H_q
\]

is an isomorphism and

\[
H=\{(x,\theta(x)):x\in H_p\}.
\]

Thus \(H\) is the graph of an isomorphism.

## 2. Exact positive-separator count in a supergroup

Because \(G\) is abelian, all relevant quotients are groups. Projection
induces a surjection

\[
\overline\pi_p:K/H\longrightarrow K_p/H_p.
\]

The source and target have orders \(c\) and \(a\), respectively. Therefore
\(a\mid c\), and the kernel has order \(c/a\). Similarly, \(b\mid c\).

For the exact count, use the unquotiented projection. Since

\[
|K|=ch,
\qquad
|K_p|=ah,
\qquad
|K_q|=bh,
\]

the homomorphism theorem gives

\[
|\ker(\pi_p|_K)|=\frac ca,
\qquad
|\ker(\pi_q|_K)|=\frac cb.
\]

After the identity is removed, every element of the first kernel is equal to
\(1\) in the \(p\)-component and unequal to \(1\) in the \(q\)-component.
There are \(c/a-1\) such positive separators. The second kernel similarly
contributes \(c/b-1\). These two sets are disjoint, because an element equal
to \(1\) in both components is the identity. Thus the exact number is

\[
\frac ca+\frac cb-2.
\]

Uniform division by \(|K|=ch\) gives density

\[
\delta_+(K)=\frac{c/a+c/b-2}{ch}.
\]

Since \(c/a\) and \(c/b\) are positive integers, this count is nonzero
exactly when \(c/a>1\) or \(c/b>1\), equivalently when \(c>a\) or \(c>b\).

## 3. Splitting one old block

Let \(\beta\in H\) be the residue of \(B\). Since \(B=uv\) and \(B\) is a
unit, both \(u\) and \(v\) represent elements of \(G\), and

\[
v=\beta u^{-1}\in\langle H,u\rangle.
\]

It follows that

\[
K=\langle H,u,v\rangle=\langle H,u\rangle.
\]

Therefore \(K/H\) is cyclic, generated by \(uH\). Its order is

\[
c=\operatorname{ord}(uH).
\]

Likewise,

\[
K_p/H_p=\langle u_pH_p\rangle,
\qquad
K_q/H_q=\langle u_qH_q\rangle,
\]

so

\[
a=\operatorname{ord}(u_pH_p),
\qquad
b=\operatorname{ord}(u_qH_q).
\]

These are the global and two local coset orders of \(u\).

Every coset of \(H\) in \(K\) is exactly one of
\(H,uH,\ldots,u^{c-1}H\). Hence every element of \(K\) has a representation

\[
u^tz,
\qquad 0\le t<c,
\qquad z\in H.
\]

If \(u^tz=u^{t'}z'\), then \(u^{t-t'}\in H\). The order definition forces
\(c\mid t-t'\). The chosen exponent range gives \(t=t'\), followed by
\(z=z'\). The representation is unique.

This representation describes both local kernels exactly. For the
\(p\)-projection, an exponent can occur precisely when

\[
u_p^t\in H_p,
\]

which is equivalent to \(a\mid t\). For each such exponent there is one and
only one old element

\[
z_{p,t}=(\pi_p|_H)^{-1}(u_p^{-t})\in H.
\]

Thus

\[
\ker(\pi_p|_K)
=\{u^{ja}z_{p,ja}:0\le j<c/a\}.
\]

The term \(j=0\) is the identity. The other \(c/a-1\) terms are exactly the
positive separators with \(p\)-component \(1\). Similarly,

\[
z_{q,t}=(\pi_q|_H)^{-1}(u_q^{-t})
\]

exists exactly when \(b\mid t\), and

\[
\ker(\pi_q|_K)
=\{u^{jb}z_{q,jb}:0\le j<c/b\}.
\]

This also displays the local quotient kernels: their exponent classes are
exactly the multiples of \(a\) and \(b\), respectively. The elements
\(z_{p,t}\) and \(z_{q,t}\) are the unique old cancellation elements for
those exponents.

## 4. Direct-sign density in cosets

For a unit \(x=(x_p,x_q)\), \(\gcd(x-1,N)\) is a proper factor exactly when
one, but not both, of \(x_p,x_q\) equals \(1\). Likewise,
\(\gcd(x+1,N)\) is a proper factor exactly when one, but not both, of the
components equals \(-1\).

Fix a coset \(gH\). Since each projection is injective on \(H\), it is also
injective on \(gH\). Consequently, each of the four equations

\[
x_p=1,
\qquad x_q=1,
\qquad x_p=-1,
\qquad x_q=-1
\]

has at most one solution in \(gH\). Every direct-sign success satisfies at
least one of these equations. Thus at most four elements of \(gH\) can give
a proper factor through either sign. Some candidates can coincide or can
have both components equal to the same sign; this only decreases the number
of successful elements.

The uniform law on a coset of size \(h\) therefore has direct-sign success
probability at most \(4/h\). An \(H\)-invariant law is a convex mixture of
such laws, so it has the same upper bound.

For one fixed sign there are only two local equations. Hence every
\(H\)-coset has at most two positive-sign candidates and at most two
negative-sign candidates. Uniform sampling from any \(K\ge H\) is a uniform
mixture over its \(H\)-cosets, and therefore

\[
\delta_+(K)\le\frac2h,
\qquad
\delta_-(K)\le\frac2h.
\]

The first inequality also follows directly from the exact formula in Claim
2, because \(c/a\le c\) and \(c/b\le c\).

## 5. Adaptive trials close to invariant laws

Let \(S\subseteq G\) be the direct-sign success set. For a realized history
before trial \(t\), let \(\mu_t\) be the actual conditional law and let
\(\nu_t\) be the stated \(H\)-invariant comparison law. Total variation
controls every event, so

\[
\mu_t(S)
\le \nu_t(S)+\|\mu_t-\nu_t\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
\le \frac4h+\varepsilon_t. \tag{1}
\]

This holds for every history, including histories selected by earlier
failures. Sum the disjoint first-success events, or apply the union bound and
average (1) over prior histories. The result is

\[
\Pr(\text{some direct-sign success in \(T\) trials})
\le \sum_{t=1}^T\left(\frac4h+\varepsilon_t\right)
=\frac{4T}{h}+\sum_{t=1}^T\varepsilon_t.
\]

No independence between trials is required.

## 6. Products and quotients with an independent uniform factor

Fix signs \(\sigma,\tau\in\{1,-1\}\). Inversion permutes \(H\), so
\(X^\sigma\) is uniform on \(H\). Conditional on \(Y=y\), independence
therefore makes

\[
X^\sigma Y^\tau
\]

uniform on the coset \(Hy^\tau\). Averaging over \(y\) gives a mixture of
uniform \(H\)-coset laws. Thus each of the four fixed-sign laws
\(X^{\pm1}Y^{\pm1}\) is \(H\)-invariant.

This controls the following scans:

* A fixed partner \(y\), fixed pair of indices, and fixed signs are covered.
* A predictable partner and predictable signs are covered when, conditional
  on the prior history, the fresh \(X\) remains uniform on \(H\) and is
  independent of that choice.
* A partner selected from a pool independent of \(X\) is covered if the
  selection rule uses only that pool and information independent of \(X\).
  A predeclared scan of many such pairs is also covered pair by pair; a union
  bound does not require those pair values to be mutually independent.

The statement does not cover a partner, index, or sign selected using the
realized value of \(X\), using both realized operands, or using the resulting
pair values. Such selection can destroy conditional uniformity and
independence and can concentrate the selected product or quotient on a small
set. An arbitrary value-dependent pair scan needs a separate invariant-law
or total-variation argument.

## 7. The cyclic density transition

Use additive notation for \(C_L\), so the identity is \(0\) and a separator
has exactly one zero coordinate. The assumptions \(u\ne0\) and \(v\ne0\)
show immediately that \(z=(u,v)\) is not a separator.

Consider the surjective homomorphism

\[
d:C_L\times C_L\longrightarrow C_L,
\qquad
d(x,y)=y-x.
\]

Its kernel is the diagonal subgroup \(H\). Since

\[
d(z)=v-u
\]

generates \(C_L\), the subgroup \(\langle H,z\rangle\) has full image under
\(d\) and contains its kernel. Therefore

\[
\langle H,z\rangle=C_L\times C_L.
\]

In the full product, the positive separators are

\[
\{(0,t):t\ne0\}\ \cup\ \{(t,0):t\ne0\}.
\]

The two sets are disjoint and each has \(L-1\) elements. Dividing their
total size by \(L^2\) gives density

\[
\frac{2L-2}{L^2}.
\]

## Scope decision

The hypothesis that \(H\) contains no positive separator concerns every
element of \(H\). Failure to find a separator with a finite test menu does
not establish that hypothesis; an untested element can still be a separator.

The density bounds concern only the direct tests \(\gcd(x-1,N)\) and
\(\gcd(x+1,N)\). They do not bound exact square-class decoding, direct
integer-overlap extraction, or any other observation that is not one of
these two sign tests.

Claim 7 is an abstract cyclic-group example. It proves a possible subgroup
and density transition. It does not assert that the example is realized by
canonical inverses, integer blocks, or any particular sampling process.

Finally, the kernel descriptions are algebraic characterizations. They do
not provide a public procedure for finding the hidden local-order indices or
the cancellation elements. None of the claims proves a computational lower
bound, constructs a factoring algorithm, or claims that no such algorithm
exists.
