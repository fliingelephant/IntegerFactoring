# F70 refinement-gain selector boundary

## Status

Proof-only candidate. No research computation was used. The arithmetic
examples are included as exact certificates. This artifact has not yet passed
a hostile audit or proof-blind reconstruction.

## 1. Purpose

P73 and P74 give an exact decoder target for one appended feedback relation:
its square-class column must close, and its induced root must be non-global.
P76 shows a different possible gain: the canonical inverse endpoint can split
an old composite gcd-free block and thereby enlarge the subgroup generated by
the refined blocks.

This note asks whether the public amount of integer refinement is itself a
selector. The answer is mixed.

- Strict refinement is a real algorithmic state change.
- It has a finite transcript potential.
- It can enlarge a subgroup that previously had no direct separator into one
  that contains a separator.
- Positive old-block splitting is neither necessary nor sufficient for
  immediate success across the two displayed histories.
- Subgroup enlargement does not by itself provide a legal representative in
  the current finite occurrence box.

No all-input selector follows.

## 2. Exact model

Let \(N>1\) be odd. At one stage, let

\[
Q=\{q_1,\ldots,q_r\}
\]

be the current pairwise-coprime nontrivial gcd-free integer blocks. Every
block is a unit modulo \(N\). Let

\[
H(Q)=\langle q_1,\ldots,q_r\rangle
\subseteq(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times
\]

be their modular subgroup.

A legal feedback candidate is an exact positive block product \(g\), within
the declared indexed occurrence capacities, with

\[
1<g<N.
\]

Let \(w\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}\) be its canonical inverse and append

\[
A_*=gw=1+kN.
\]

Jointly gcd-refine every old endpoint and the two new endpoints. Existing
blocks never merge. They can stay intact or split into pairwise-coprime
descendants. New factors of \(w\) that divide no old block are private new
blocks.

Define:

- \(\sigma\): the increase in the number of descendants of old block types;
- \(\nu\): the number of private new block types.

Thus, if the old and new complete block counts are \(r\) and \(r'\), then

\[
r'=r+\sigma+\nu.
\tag{1}
\]

The values \(\sigma,\nu\) are factor-free and public. Ordinary integer gcds
and exact square tests compute them. They say nothing by themselves about
membership in \(H(Q)\), because modular subgroup membership can contain the
order-finding problem from P71.

The immediate public screens precede any ranking. For an inverse pair
\(gw\equiv1\pmod N\), the declared screens are

\[
\gcd(g-1,N),\quad \gcd(g+1,N),\quad
\gcd(g-w,N),\quad
\gcd((g-w)^2+4,N),
\]

together with the exact integer-square test on \(gw\). The sign screens on
\(w\) are equivalent to those on \(g\). The fourth gcd is the
inverse-pair discriminant screen. If these tests fail, P73 and P74 then test
square-class closure and its induced root.

## 3. Finite transcript potential

Consider any fixed finite final transcript of positive endpoint integers.
Let

\[
L=\sum_x\lceil\log_2(x+1)\rceil
\]

be its total endpoint encoding length. If its complete gcd-free refinement
has \(R\) distinct nontrivial block types, then

\[
R\le L.
\tag{2}
\]

Indeed, all distinct block types are pairwise coprime and at least \(2\).
Their product divides the product of the endpoint integers. Taking binary
logarithms proves (2).

For any insertion order leading to that fixed transcript, (1) telescopes:

\[
\sum_t(\sigma_t+\nu_t)=R-r_0\le L-r_0.
\tag{3}
\]

In particular,

\[
\sum_t\sigma_t\le L-r_0.
\tag{4}
\]

This is only an accounting bound. In an adaptive process, every round appends
another \(O(\log N)\)-bit endpoint, so \(L\) grows with the number of rounds.
Equation (4) does not prove a polynomial round bound. A process can add one
private block per round for a number of rounds not bounded polynomially by
(4).

## 4. A separator-free old subgroup at \(N=4033\)

Put

\[
N=4033=37\cdot109=\Phi_{36}(2).
\]

Both prime factors give order \(36\) for \(2\). Exact certificates are

\[
2^{18}\equiv-1\pmod {37},
\qquad
2^{12}\equiv26\not\equiv1\pmod {37},
\]

and

\[
2^{18}\equiv-1\pmod {109},
\qquad
2^{12}\equiv63\not\equiv1\pmod {109}.
\]

Since the order divides \(36\), these identities force

\[
\operatorname{ord}_{37}(2)
=\operatorname{ord}_{109}(2)=36.
\]

Consequently the diagonal subgroup

\[
H_0=\langle2\rangle\subseteq(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times
\]

has no direct separator. For every exponent \(e\),

\[
2^e\equiv1\pmod {37}
\Longleftrightarrow
2^e\equiv1\pmod {109},
\]

and the same equivalence holds for \(-1\), at exponent
\(e\equiv18\pmod {36}\). Therefore every \(h\in H_0\) has

\[
\gcd(h-1,N),\ \gcd(h+1,N)\in\{1,N\}.
\tag{5}
\]

This is stronger than the old subgroup in P76's \(N=209\) witness, which
already contained another separator.

## 5. An infinite immediate-closure family

Let \(s>1\) be odd with

\[
s\equiv1\pmod3,
\]

and define

\[
N_s=\frac{4s^2-1}{3}
=
(2s-1)\frac{2s+1}{3}.
\tag{6}
\]

The two displayed factors are odd, greater than one, and coprime. A common
divisor divides their combination \(2\), so it must be one.

Also

\[
N_s+1
=
2\frac{2s^2+1}{3}.
\]

Retain two separately indexed copies of this quotient-one relation. Their
complete old decoder has only the duplicate dependency, whose positive root
is \(N_s+1\equiv1\pmod {N_s}\). The two charged copies make

\[
g=2^2=4
\]

a legal occurrence product. Its canonical inverse is

\[
w=s^2,
\]

because

\[
4s^2=1+3N_s=(2s)^2.
\tag{7}
\]

The inequalities \(1<s^2<N_s\) hold for \(s>1\), so this is the canonical
integer endpoint. The appended relation is an exact square with non-global
root \(2s\). In fact,

\[
\gcd(2s-1,N_s)=2s-1,
\]

and

\[
\gcd(2s+1,N_s)=\frac{2s+1}{3}.
\tag{8}
\]

Thus two charged copies and one fixed candidate factor every member of this
special composite family.

The earlier direct and discriminant tickets can be forced to fail on an
infinite subfamily. Take

\[
s\equiv55\pmod {1530}.
\]

Then \(s\equiv1\pmod9\), \(s\equiv0\pmod5\), and
\(s\equiv4\pmod {17}\). Direct substitution into (6) gives

\[
\gcd(N_s,3\cdot5\cdot17)=1.
\]

Therefore \(g=4\) passes both sign screens and the
inverse-pair discriminant screen before (7) supplies the useful square.
Indeed,

\[
g^2\bigl((g-w)^2+4\bigr)
\equiv(g^2+1)^2=17^2\pmod {N_s},
\]

and both \(g\) and \(17\) are units modulo \(N_s\).

This is an infinite special-family theorem, not an all-input probability
law. The parameter \(s\) is recovered by the same exact square test, so the
family does not give a factoring lower bound.

### A separator-free instance

Take two separately indexed copies of

\[
A_1=A_2=N+1=4034=2\cdot2017.
\]

The old blocks are \(2,2017\), with

\[
2017\equiv2^{-1}\pmod N.
\]

They generate exactly \(H_0\). The two old parity columns are equal. Their
only new dependency has positive root \(A_1\equiv1\pmod N\), so the complete
old decoder gives only the global identity.

The two charged copies make

\[
g=2^2=4
\]

a legal occurrence product. Its direct and discriminant screens fail:

\[
\gcd(4-1,N)=\gcd(4+1,N)=\gcd(4^2+1,N)=1.
\]

Its canonical inverse is

\[
w=3025=55^2,
\]

because

\[
4\cdot3025=12100=1+3N=110^2.
\]

Thus the appended relation is an exact integer square. Its singleton root is
non-global:

\[
110\equiv-1\pmod {37},
\qquad
110\equiv1\pmod {109}.
\]

Both sign screens factor:

\[
\gcd(110-1,N)=109,
\qquad
\gcd(110+1,N)=37.
\tag{9}
\]

No old block splits: \(\sigma=0\). The new perfect-square endpoint passes
P73's closure gate and P74's root-label gate immediately. It also introduces
a private new block, so the total block gain is positive. The exact conclusion
is that positive old-block splitting, \(\sigma>0\), is not necessary for useful
feedback. This uses two explicitly charged occurrences and gives no all-input
frequency law.

## 6. Strict subgroup expansion from three distinct relations

Retain the three exact inverse relations

\[
2\cdot2017=4034=1+N,
\tag{10}
\]

\[
64\cdot3970=254080=1+63N,
\tag{11}
\]

and

\[
8\cdot3529=28232=1+7N.
\tag{12}
\]

Their complete old gcd-free blocks are

\[
Q_0=\{2,2017,1985,3529\},
\]

because \(3970=2\cdot1985\). Direct Euclidean gcds show that the three odd
blocks are pairwise coprime. Equations (10)--(12) give

\[
2017\equiv2^{-1},
\qquad
1985\equiv2^{-7},
\qquad
3529\equiv2^{-3}\pmod N.
\]

Hence

\[
H(Q_0)=H_0=\langle2\rangle,
\]

which has no direct separator by (5).

The total available exponent of the block \(2\) is

\[
1+7+3=11.
\]

Select the legal cross-relation product

\[
g=2^{11}=2048<N.
\]

Its canonical inverse is

\[
w=3905,
\]

and the exact feedback relation is

\[
2048\cdot3905
=7\,997\,440
=1+1983N.
\tag{13}
\]

Both endpoints remain in \(H_0\):

\[
g=2^{11},
\qquad
w\equiv2^{-11}=2^{25}\pmod N.
\]

The immediate screens all fail:

\[
\gcd(2048\pm1,N)=1,
\qquad
\gcd(3905\pm1,N)=1,
\]

and, since \(2048-3905=-1857\),

\[
\gcd(1857^2+4,N)=1.
\]

The product in (13) is nonsquare because

\[
2048\cdot3905=2^{11}\cdot5\cdot781.
\]

The only overlap that properly splits an old block is

\[
\gcd(1985,3905)=5.
\]

There is also the full containment \(\gcd(2,2048)=2\), because the selected
endpoint deliberately reuses the whole old block \(2\). It does not refine
that block.

It splits

\[
1985=5\cdot397,
\qquad
3905=5\cdot781,
\]

and gives the exact refined block set

\[
Q_1=\{2,2017,5,397,3529,781\}.
\tag{14}
\]

Thus \(\sigma=1\) and \(\nu=1\). The private block \(781\) has odd
multiplicity in the new relation and zero multiplicity in every old column.
By P73, the new square-class column cannot close. The step does not factor
immediately and adds no new decoder dependency.

It does, however, strictly enlarge the block-generated subgroup. Every block
in (14) lies in

\[
H_1=\langle H_0,5\rangle.
\]

Modulo \(37\),

\[
5=2^{23}.
\]

Modulo \(109\),

\[
2^{23}=2^{18}2^5\equiv-32\equiv77\ne5.
\]

The equal order \(36\) at both primes shows that \(5\notin H_0\). Hence

\[
H_0\subsetneq H_1.
\tag{15}
\]

Moreover, \(H_1\) contains a direct separator. Since
\(2^{-23}=2^{13}\) in \(H_0\), put

\[
x=5\cdot2^{-23}\equiv5\cdot2^{13}\equiv630\pmod N.
\]

Then

\[
x\equiv1\pmod {37},
\qquad
x\equiv85\pmod {109},
\]

and

\[
\gcd(x-1,N)=\gcd(629,4033)=37.
\tag{16}
\]

This is a genuine subgroup-level gain. The old subgroup contained no direct
separator, while the refined subgroup does.

## 7. The finite-box caveat

Equation (16) is not an immediate factoring algorithm. The displayed
subgroup representation uses

\[
5\cdot2^{13}=40960>N.
\]

Its canonical residue \(630=2\cdot3^2\cdot5\cdot7\) uses the absent exact
integer factors \(3\) and \(7\). In fact, the current legal occurrence box
cannot contain this residue under the declared positive whole-block rule. The
canonical representative below \(N\) is unique. None of
\(2017,397,3529,781\) supplies a factor \(3\) or \(7\), and products of the
available whole blocks \(2\) and \(5\) cannot equal \(630\). The larger
representation \(5\cdot2^{13}=40960\) reaches \(630\) only after modular
reduction, which is a different source operation. This is an instance of the
bounded-representative obstruction highlighted by P71, not an exact
equivalence theorem from P71.

Therefore (15)--(16) prove strict expansion to a factor-bearing subgroup
element. They do not prove that the declared next finite menu reaches that
element in polynomial time.

## 8. Old-block split count is not a stand-alone certificate

The two exact histories prove separate one-way failures.

- Section 5 has \(\sigma=0\) and gives an immediate non-global closure.
  Positive old-block splitting is therefore not necessary for useful
  feedback.
- Section 6 has \(\sigma=1\), but it fails every immediate screen and its
  private odd row prevents closure. Positive old-block splitting is therefore
  not sufficient for an immediate factor or useful root.

This is not a same-history ordering counterexample. If the old relations from
both sections are merged, the endpoint \(3025\) also overlaps the old block
\(1985\) through the factor \(5\), so its split score changes. No claim is
made here against a rule that enumerates one fixed candidate menu, runs all
screens, and then ranks the remaining candidates. In particular, the two
histories do not prove a same-menu monotonicity theorem and do not rule out
using \(\sigma\) together with block identities, multiplicities, or later
feedback.

Private-block count is also not progress toward closure. An odd private row
certifies the opposite: the new column is outside the old square-class span.

## 9. Surviving algorithmic target

The two \(N=4033\) witnesses establish a real mechanism-level distinction.

1. Canonical feedback can create a non-global closure even when the complete
   old block subgroup has no direct separator.
2. Canonical feedback can split an old composite block and strictly enlarge a
   separator-free subgroup into one containing a separator, even though both
   feedback endpoint residues stay in the old subgroup.

Neither result supplies the missing sampler. A full factoring theorem still
needs a public rule that, for every input, produces only polynomially many
legal candidates or a polynomial-length trajectory and reaches one of:

- an immediate direct separator;
- a closure with non-global induced root;
- a strict subgroup enlargement together with a legal, efficiently found
  factor-bearing representative.

The rule must have inverse-polynomial success probability and uniform
polynomial bit complexity. Equations (3)--(4) are not such a rule.

**Classification:** keep canonical endpoint feedback as a live source
mechanism. Raw old-block split count is not a stand-alone certificate of
immediate factoring success or useful square-class closure. Its value as one
feature in a uniform selector remains open.
