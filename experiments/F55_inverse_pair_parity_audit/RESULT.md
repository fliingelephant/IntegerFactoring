## 1. Decisive mathematical error: the decoder can create a new root value

Equation (2.2) correctly gives

\[
X_S\equiv\prod_z z^{h_z}\pmod N,
\]

where the product ranges over self-inverse labels in the batch. This product
need not equal any one label in the batch, and it need not be \(\pm1\).

An exact counterexample is \(N=105\). Both

\[
29^2\equiv1\pmod {105},\qquad 41^2\equiv1\pmod {105}.
\]

Take one copy of each loop, \((29,29)\) and \((41,41)\). Each loop has even
endpoint parity by itself. Selecting both gives

\[
X_S\equiv29\cdot41\equiv34\pmod {105},
\qquad 34^2\equiv1\pmod {105}.
\]

But

\[
34\not\equiv\pm29,\ \pm41,\ \pm1\pmod {105}.
\]

Thus the pooled decoder has produced a new nontrivial root value. This
directly contradicts these claims:

- the introduction says that the formal parity matrix has a “trivial kernel
  image modulo \(N\)”;
- Section 2 says that the decoder “never creates a new nontrivial square
  root” and “returns a root already exposed by one self-loop”;
- Section 4 says that it produces only “direct self-loop roots or
  \(\pm1\).”

The intended *factoring-information* conclusion survives. In this example,

\[
\gcd(29-1,105)=7,
\qquad
\gcd(41-1,105)=5.
\]

Therefore either loop already factors \(N\) before pooling. The correct claim
is:

> The nonloop part of every formal-parity kernel vector contributes \(1\).
> The full output lies in the subgroup generated by submitted self-loop
> labels. If direct screening of every such label does not factor \(N\), then
> all such labels are \(\pm1\), and every pooled output is \(\pm1\).

This is enough to prove that the decoder gives no new factoring power. It is
not enough to claim that its raw kernel image is trivial or that it cannot
produce a new root value.

## 2. The graph-cycle sentence is false under the candidate's own model

The candidate explicitly permits repeated relations. The resulting object is
a multigraph, not a simple matching. Two parallel copies of one nonloop
inverse pair form a nonzero binary cycle-space vector. Each self-loop is also
a zero incidence column and hence a cycle-space generator in the usual binary
incidence convention.

Thus the sentence that the graph “has no nontrivial cycles” is false as
written. The proof establishes a different and useful fact:

- the underlying *simple nonloop* graph is a disjoint union of edges;
- every binary kernel vector on a nonloop orbit selects an even number of its
  parallel edges;
- the decoded contribution of each such vector is \(1\bmod N\);
- loop columns supply only the already public self-inverse labels.

The closure comes from the value of those kernel generators, not from the
absence of multigraph cycles. The sentence must also stay limited to the
formal endpoint-incidence decoder. A graph construction that uses integer
vertex values, chronology, or extra relations is outside the proof.

## 4. Checks that passed

### Endpoint parity and loops

The definitions are correct. For a two-element inversion orbit

\[
\{x,\iota(x)\},
\]

every selected relation contributes one occurrence of each endpoint. Even
endpoint parity therefore forces an even number \(2h\) of selected relations,
and its visible-root contribution is

\[
(x\iota(x))^h\equiv1\pmod N.
\]

A loop \((z,z)\) contributes two occurrences of \(z\). It automatically has
zero binary incidence, and one selected loop contributes \(z\) to \(X_S\).
Equation (2.2) follows exactly. Orienting a nonloop relation in either order
does not change this proof.

### Odd-composite gcd screen, including prime powers

Let \(z^2\equiv1\pmod N\), with odd

\[
N=\prod_i p_i^{\alpha_i}.
\]

For every \(p_i^{\alpha_i}\), the congruence

\[
(z-1)(z+1)\equiv0\pmod {p_i^{\alpha_i}}
\]

and \(\gcd(z-1,z+1)\mid2\) imply that the full prime power divides exactly one
of \(z-1\) and \(z+1\). Hence a mixed assignment of signs gives a proper
factor through one of the two gcds. If neither gcd is proper and nontrivial,
all prime-power components use the same sign, so \(z\equiv1\) or
\(z\equiv-1\pmod N\). This includes odd prime powers, for which these are the
only two roots. The candidate's direct-screen argument is correct.

### The \(N=15\) arithmetic counterexample

The example is exact:

\[
2^{-1}\equiv8\pmod {15},
\qquad
2\cdot8=16=4^2,
\qquad
\gcd(4-1,15)=3.
\]

The single nonloop edge has nonzero formal endpoint incidence. Its product is
an integer square because the prime valuations of \(2\) and \(8\) cancel
modulo two. It correctly proves that formal label parity is strictly more
restrictive than true arithmetic square parity.

### Exact arithmetic-matrix distinction

The prose has the right idea but should define the matrix explicitly. If
\(C\) is the public label-incidence matrix and \(V\) maps each label to its
rational-prime valuation vector modulo two, then

\[
\ker C\subseteq\ker(VC).
\]

Formal pooling searches \(\ker C\). True square-class pooling searches
\(\ker(VC)\). Equivalently, the column for relation \(j\) in the arithmetic
matrix is

\[
[a_j]+[b_j]=[a_jb_j]
\quad\text{in }\mathbb Q^\times/(\mathbb Q^\times)^2.
\]

For positive integer products, a zero rational square class is exactly an
integer square. The \(N=15\) example lies in
\(\ker(VC)\setminus\ker C\). Defining these maps would remove the current
ambiguity in the phrase “this arithmetic matrix.”

### Adaptivity and general scope

The orbit calculation is pointwise. It remains valid for a random or adaptive
finite batch and for any method that chooses a vector in the formal incidence
kernel, provided every submitted pair still satisfies
\(b_j=\iota(a_j)\). No independence or distributional assumption is used.

This does **not** cover a decoder that uses the integer sizes of labels, the
quotients \(k_j\), chronological links between descent steps, arithmetic
factor-base relations, nonlinear functions of the transcript, or added
relations between different inversion orbits. The candidate mostly preserves
this boundary.

## 5. Comparison with the promoted boundaries

### P01 and X01

The comparison is correct after the arithmetic matrix is defined as above.
P01/X01 forbid rank reduction by a universally sound exact linear sketch for
arbitrary square classes. They do not forbid a special descent source whose
true square-class span is small, whose endpoints are smooth, or whose
dependencies are otherwise easy to recognize. F55 must not promote its formal
incidence collapse into an obstruction to arithmetic square-class pooling.
The candidate explicitly leaves that route open.

### P62 and F26

P62 supplies the relations

\[
u_i v_i=N u_{i+1}+1,
\qquad v_i=\iota(u_i).
\]

F55 correctly analyzes only the inverse-pair projection
\((u_i,v_i)\). It discards \(u_{i+1}\), integer magnitude, and chronology.
It proves no depth bound, hit law, sampler, or factoring algorithm. The stated
conclusion that F26 remains open is correct.

### Hidden-period, HSP, and stabilizer ideas

The proof does not analyze hidden-subgroup states, Fourier sampling, Bayesian
networks, stabilizer simulation, or Shor's circuit. Naming a hidden-period or
stabilizer retry does not close it. After repair, the candidate must keep the
claim precise: only a decoder that uses the formal endpoint-incidence kernel
and no extra transcript structure is closed.
