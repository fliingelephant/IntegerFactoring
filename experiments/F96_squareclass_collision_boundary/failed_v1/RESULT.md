# F96 — square-class collision boundary at \(N=2773\)

**Status:** exact bounded theorem candidate. It is not audited or promoted.
It is not an all-input factoring algorithm.

## 1. Main result

F95 contains two different facts. They must not be merged.

1. Equal nonzero integer square classes create a congruence of squares.
   They give a factor only when the two normalized roots differ by more than
   one global sign.
2. At \(N=2773\), the F95 endpoint \(842\) is outside the residue subgroup
   generated by the first normalized relation. However, the **same second
   relation value** has another canonical-inverse presentation inside that
   subgroup.

The second fact gives a real bounded selector:

\[
c=[3^{99}43]_{2773}=1263,
\qquad
w=c^{-1}_{\rm can}=1684,
\]

and

\[
cw=1263\cdot1684=2{,}126{,}892
=842\cdot2526=P_2.
\]

Thus canonical-residue feedback from the first state can reproduce the F95
closing relation without knowing \(842\). Ordinary integer-product feedback
cannot do this in one step.

## 2. Exact square-class theorem

Let \(N\) be odd. Let

\[
P_i=g_iw_i\equiv1\pmod N
\]

be two positive canonical-inverse relation values. Assume that neither
\(P_i\) is an integer square. The following statements are equivalent:

1. \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) have the same nonzero class in
   \(\mathbb Q_{>0}^{\times}/(\mathbb Q_{>0}^{\times})^2\).
2. \(P_1P_2\) is an exact integer square.
3. For
   \(D=\gcd(P_1,P_2)\), there are coprime positive integers \(A,B\) such that

   \[
   P_1=DA^2,
   \qquad
   P_2=DB^2,
   \]

   and \(D\) is not a square.

This normalization is public. It needs one gcd, two exact divisions, and
two exact square-root tests. It does not need the factors of \(N\).

Because \(D\) is a unit modulo \(N\), the two relations give

\[
A^2\equiv B^2\pmod N.
\]

Define

\[
R=\sqrt{P_1P_2}=DAB.
\]

Then \(R^2\equiv1\pmod N\), and the following identities are exact:

\[
\boxed{\gcd(R-1,N)=\gcd(A-B,N)},
\]

\[
\boxed{\gcd(R+1,N)=\gcd(A+B,N)}.
\]

Indeed,

\[
DB(A-B)=R-P_2\equiv R-1\pmod N,
\]

and \(DB\) is a unit. The plus identity is the same calculation with
\(A+B\).

Therefore equal nonzero square class is equivalent to an **exactly
normalized root collision**. It is not equivalent to successful
factorization. For odd \(N\), the collision factors \(N\) exactly when

\[
R\not\equiv\pm1\pmod N,
\]

or, equivalently, when \(A\not\equiv\pm B\pmod N\) globally.

### Exact counterexample to automatic factorization

The bounded source found the first counterexample in its declared distinct
odd-semiprime scan at

\[
N=143=11\cdot13.
\]

The canonical-inverse values are

\[
102\cdot136=13{,}872=3\cdot68^2,
\]

\[
125\cdot135=16{,}875=3\cdot75^2.
\]

They have the same nonzero square class, but \(68+75=143\). Hence the
induced root is \(-1\) modulo \(143\), and the two gcds are \(1\) and \(143\).
No proper factor results.

This refutes the unconditional statement

\[
\text{same nonzero square class}\Longrightarrow\text{factor}.
\]

The missing condition is exactly the non-global-sign condition above.

## 3. The endpoint \(842\) is outside the first subgroup

For F95,

\[
P_1=3\cdot1849=3\cdot43^2=1+2N.
\]

The public normalized blocks are \(3\) and \(43\). Let

\[
H=\langle3,43\rangle\subseteq(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times.
\]

The exact computation gives

\[
\operatorname{ord}_N(3)=667,
\qquad
\operatorname{ord}_N(43)=1334,
\qquad
|H|=1334.
\]

There is also a short public nonmembership certificate. The first relation
gives \(3=43^{-2}\), so \(H=\langle43\rangle\) is cyclic. Moreover,

\[
43^{667}\equiv-1\pmod N.
\]

But

\[
t=842\cdot43^{-1}\equiv471\pmod N,
\qquad
t^2\equiv1\pmod N,
\qquad
t\not\equiv\pm1\pmod N.
\]

A cyclic group has only one element of order two. Here that element is
\(-1\). Therefore \(t\notin H\), and hence

\[
\boxed{842\notin H}.
\]

Its inverse \(2526\) is also outside \(H\). The complete public subgroup
BFS confirms both nonmembership claims. That BFS costs \(O(N)\), so it is a
finite certificate search, not a general polynomial-time membership
algorithm.

## 4. The relation is reachable even though the endpoint is not

The endpoint question is too strict. The decoder uses the relation value,
not one fixed factorization of it.

The complete scan of \(H\) found exactly two residues whose canonical
inverse relation has the same nonzero square class as \(P_1\). They are an
inverse pair:

\[
1263^{-1}\equiv1684\pmod N.
\]

Both give the same relation value \(P_2\). Their shortest words under the
declared searches are

\[
1263\equiv3^{99}43
       \equiv3^{98}43^{-1}\pmod N,
\]

\[
1684\equiv43^{197}
       \equiv3^{-98}43\pmod N.
\]

Thus a uniform residue from \(H\) hits a closing presentation with exact
probability

\[
\frac{2}{1334}=\frac1{667}
\]

on this fixed input. This is one finite density. It is not a general density
law.

The positive word \(3^{99}43\) is much larger than \(N\). Thus this is
canonical-residue feedback: first reduce the word modulo \(N\), and then
take the canonical inverse. It is not ordinary feedback from a raw integer
product below \(N\).

The resulting relation closes with \(P_1\):

\[
\sqrt{P_1P_2}=108{,}618,
\]

\[
\gcd(108{,}618-1,2773)=47,
\qquad
\gcd(108{,}618+1,2773)=59.
\]

## 5. Selector status

Three search levels give different conclusions.

### Ordinary integer-product feedback: no

The source exhausts all twelve products

\[
3^a43^b<N,
\qquad a,b\ge0,
\qquad (a,b)\ne(0,0).
\]

None reaches endpoint \(842\), relation value \(P_2\), or any other
same-square-class closure with \(P_1\).

With the exact occurrence limits of the first relation
\(a\le1,\ b\le2\), the four legal candidates only reproduce \(P_1\).

### Canonical-residue menu with exponents at most \(n\): no

For \(n=\lceil\log_2N\rceil=12\), the blind menu

\[
0\le a,b\le n
\]

tests 37 distinct residues. It finds no closure.

### Canonical-residue menu with exponents at most \(n^2\): yes

The blind menu

\[
0\le a,b\le n^2=144
\]

orders candidates by increasing \(a+b\), then increasing \(a\). For every
candidate it uses only the first public state. It forms the canonical
inverse relation and tests whether its product with \(P_1\) is an exact
square.

This search finds the relation \(P_2\) at \((a,b)=(99,1)\). It is exponent
pair 5150 and distinct residue 299 in the declared order. It then factors
\(N\) by the public root gcds above. The menu contains \(145^2=21{,}025\)
exponent pairs, so this fixed two-block search is polynomial in \(n\).

This is a genuine **bounded public selector for this instance**. It does not
receive \(842\), \(P_2\), or a factor of \(N\) as input. It is therefore more
than a restatement of “find the second root.”

However, the exponent bound \(n^2\) has no all-input success theorem. The
word was first located by a complete \(O(N)\) subgroup scan. The finite
success can be post-selected. We do not know whether every hard input has a
closing word of polynomial support and polynomial exponent, or whether such
words have inverse-polynomial density.

## 6. What is public and what is certificate-only

Public polynomial-time operations are:

- gcd-free square normalization of a supplied relation pair;
- verification of the words above;
- the fixed two-block \(n^2\) menu;
- exact square tests; and
- the final gcd tests.

Certificate-only discovery operations in this experiment are:

- BFS over all \(1334\) elements of \(H\);
- the complete scan of all \(634\) canonical relation values at \(N=2773\);
  and
- the bounded search for the \(N=143\) counterexample.

The hidden factors \(47,59\) were not used to select the \(N=2773\)
candidates. They were outputs of the final gcds. The separate bounded
counterexample scan used public trial factorization to restrict its input
list to distinct odd semiprimes and to certify \(143=11\cdot13\). This was
a certificate-only finite scan, not a factor-free algorithm.

## 7. Algorithmic meaning

The useful object is not a special endpoint such as \(842\). It is a
relation value whose square class matches an earlier nonzero class and whose
normalized root collision is non-global.

This changes the source question:

\[
\text{find a hidden second endpoint}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{find any canonical presentation of a closing relation value}.
\]

At \(N=2773\), this relaxation is decisive. The endpoint is outside the old
subgroup, but an alternative presentation of its relation lies inside the
old subgroup and has a two-block word with exponents below \(n^2\).

The remaining gap is still a source theorem. We need an all-input law that
puts a useful closing presentation in a polynomial word menu, or gives it
inverse-polynomial sampling density. F96 proves neither. It gives one exact
instance where canonical-residue feedback supplies the missing relation by
a concrete polynomial-size search.
